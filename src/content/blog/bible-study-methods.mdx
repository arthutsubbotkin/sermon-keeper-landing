<p class="article-intro">Bible study methods are simple, repeatable ways to read a passage so you understand it and apply it, instead of reading past it. The eight most popular are SOAP, inductive study (OIA), HEAR, verse mapping, topical study, character study, the Swedish method and lectio divina. Below you'll find the steps of each, a short example and who it suits, so you can pick one and start studying the Bible today.</p>

<h2 id="soap">1. SOAP</h2>

SOAP is the most widely used daily method, and the one we recommend to beginners. Each study has four parts:

1. **Scripture:** write out the verse that stood out.
2. **Observation:** what do you notice? Who is speaking, to whom, and what words repeat?
3. **Application:** what does it mean for your life this week? Be specific.
4. **Prayer:** respond to God in a few sentences.

**Example:** Psalm 119:105, "Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path." *Observation:* a lamp lights the next step, not the whole road. *Application:* I don't need to know how the whole job search ends; I need to take today's step faithfully. *Prayer:* Lord, show me the next step and help me trust you with the rest.

**Who it's for:** anyone starting a daily habit, and anyone who wants a method that always ends in something practical.

Our complete <a href="/blog/soap-bible-study-method">SOAP Bible study method guide</a> walks through each step, with four fully worked examples (Psalm 23, John 15:5, Philippians 4:6–7 and James 1:22), a free printable template and tips for keeping a SOAP journal.

<h2 id="inductive">2. Inductive Bible study (OIA)</h2>

Inductive study means letting the text lead you to conclusions, rather than starting with your own. It has three stages:

1. **Observation: what does it say?** Ask who, what, when, where and how. Note repeated words, contrasts, lists and connecting words like "therefore" and "but".
2. **Interpretation: what did it mean?** Read the surrounding verses and chapter. Ask what the writer meant to the first readers. Use cross-references before commentaries.
3. **Application: what does it mean for me?** Turn what you learned into a belief to hold, a sin to avoid, a promise to trust or a step to take.

**Example:** Mark 1:35, "And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed." *Observation:* early, alone, praying. *Interpretation:* the verses before it (Mark 1:32–34) describe the whole city at the door the evening before, and right after it (1:37) the disciples say "All men seek for thee." Jesus prayed in the middle of his busiest season, not after it. *Application:* the week I'm busiest is the week I most need time alone with God.

**Who it's for:** people who want to understand a whole book of the Bible, small groups working through a book together, and anyone who likes asking questions of a text.

<h2 id="hear">3. HEAR</h2>

HEAR is a journaling method similar to SOAP, with a stronger emphasis on understanding the original meaning:

1. **Highlight** a verse that stands out to you as you read.
2. **Explain** what it meant to the original readers. What was happening, and why was it written?
3. **Apply** it to your own life.
4. **Respond** to God, usually with a written prayer or a decision.

**Example:** Matthew 11:28, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." *Explain:* Jesus is speaking to ordinary people weighed down by work and by heavy religious demands. *Apply:* I've been treating rest as something I earn after everything is done. *Respond:* I'll stop work at 9 tonight and pray instead of answering email.

**Who it's for:** people following a daily reading plan who want a bit more depth than SOAP, and anyone who tends to apply verses without first asking what they meant.

<h2 id="verse-mapping">4. Verse mapping</h2>

Verse mapping takes one verse and studies it from every angle, often on a single page with the verse in the middle and notes around it:

1. **Write the verse** in the center of the page.
2. **Define key words.** Look up two or three important words in a dictionary or a Bible dictionary.
3. **Compare translations.** Read the verse in two or three versions and note what each one brings out.
4. **Read the context.** What comes just before and after?
5. **Find cross-references.** Where else does the Bible talk about this?
6. **Summarize and apply.** Write one sentence on what the verse means and one on what you'll do.

**Example:** Hebrews 11:1, "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen." *Key words:* "substance" (something solid you can stand on), "evidence" (proof). *Context:* the rest of chapter 11 lists people who acted on what they hadn't yet seen. *Cross-reference:* 2 Corinthians 5:7. *Summary:* faith isn't wishful thinking; it's acting on what God has said before you see it. If you compare translations, our guide to <a href="/blog/bible-versions-explained">Bible versions</a> explains why the wording differs.

**Who it's for:** visual learners, detail-lovers, and anyone who wants to go deep on a verse they plan to memorize.

<h2 id="topical">5. Topical Bible study</h2>

A topical study follows one subject through the whole Bible:

1. **Pick a topic** you care about right now: peace, forgiveness, money, work, grief.
2. **Gather verses.** Use a concordance, a Bible app's search or your Bible's cross-references. Aim for 8 to 15 passages.
3. **Read each one in context**, so you don't build a teaching from a single line.
4. **Group what you find.** What does the Bible say the topic is, where it comes from and how to live it?
5. **Summarize** in a few sentences, and write one application.

**Example, peace:** Philippians 4:6–7 links peace with prayer and thanksgiving. Isaiah 26:3 promises "perfect peace" to the one "whose mind is stayed on thee". John 14:27: "Peace I leave with you, my peace I give unto you: not as the world giveth, give I unto you." *Summary:* the peace God gives comes from trusting him, not from things going well.

**Who it's for:** anyone facing a specific question or season, and people preparing to teach on a subject.

<h2 id="character">6. Character Bible study</h2>

A character study follows one person through every passage where they appear:

1. **Choose a person:** Peter, Ruth, David, Moses, Mary.
2. **List every passage** about them. A concordance or a Bible app's search makes this quick.
3. **Read them in order** and note what they did, said and felt, and how God dealt with them.
4. **Look for turning points**, strengths and failures.
5. **Ask what God teaches through their life**, and what you have in common with them.

**Example, Peter:** he walks on water and then sinks when he sees the wind (Matthew 14:28–31). He denies Jesus three times, and "went out, and wept bitterly" (Luke 22:62). After the resurrection, Jesus asks him three times, "lovest thou me?" and tells him, "Feed my sheep" (John 21:17). *Lesson:* failure wasn't the end of Peter's story; Jesus restored him and gave him work to do.

**Who it's for:** people who learn best through stories, and anyone who wants to see how faith works in a real life.

<h2 id="swedish">7. The Swedish method</h2>

The Swedish method uses three simple symbols, which makes it easy to use alone or in a group:

1. **Light bulb:** something that shines out, a new insight or something you understand better.
2. **Arrow:** something that points at you personally, a challenge or something to apply.
3. **Question mark:** something you don't understand or want to ask about.

Read the passage, mark it with the three symbols, then write one sentence for each. In a group, everyone shares their light, arrow and question, and the discussion starts from there.

**Example:** Micah 6:8, "He hath shewed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?" *Light:* God has already shown what he wants; it isn't hidden. *Arrow:* "love mercy", not just show it when I have to. *Question:* what does doing justly look like in my workplace?

**Who it's for:** small groups, families, youth groups and beginners who like something visual.

<h2 id="lectio-divina">8. Lectio divina</h2>

Lectio divina ("divine reading") is an old, slow way of reading Scripture as prayer. It's less about analysis and more about listening. It usually has four movements:

1. **Read (lectio):** read a short passage slowly, perhaps aloud. Notice a word or phrase that stands out.
2. **Reflect (meditatio):** read it again and think about that word. Why did it catch your attention?
3. **Respond (oratio):** read it a third time and talk to God about it.
4. **Rest (contemplatio):** sit quietly for a few minutes in God's presence, without words.

**Example:** Psalm 46:10, "Be still, and know that I am God." The word that stands out might be "still", and the prayer might be about how rarely you stop. Then you actually stop, for three minutes.

**Who it's for:** anyone who reads the Bible like a textbook and wants to slow down, and people who want Bible reading to lead naturally into prayer.

## How to study the Bible for beginners: which method first?

- **New to Bible study?** Start with <a href="/blog/soap-bible-study-method">SOAP</a> for two weeks.
- **Studying with a group?** Try the Swedish method or inductive study.
- **Want to understand a whole book?** Inductive study, a chapter at a time.
- **One verse keeps coming back?** Map it, then memorize it with the steps in <a href="/blog/how-to-memorize-scripture">how to memorize Scripture</a>.
- **Facing something specific?** Do a topical study.
- **Feeling dry or rushed?** Lectio divina.

You don't have to choose one forever. Many people use SOAP or HEAR every day and do a topical or character study now and then. Whatever you pick, write things down: our guides to <a href="/blog/how-to-take-bible-notes">taking Bible notes</a> and <a href="/blog/bible-journaling-for-beginners">Bible journaling for beginners</a> show how, and if a reference like "2 Cor 5:7" confuses you, see <a href="/blog/how-to-read-bible-references">how to read Bible references</a>.
