<p class="article-intro">The SOAP Bible study method has four steps: Scripture, Observation, Application, Prayer. You pick a passage, write out the verse that stood out (Scripture), note what you actually see in it (Observation), decide on one concrete way it changes your week (Application), and pray it back to God (Prayer). That's the whole method. It takes about ten minutes, and it turns reading you'd forget by lunch into something you carry into Tuesday. Below is each step, five worked SOAP examples, a free printable PDF template, how to keep a SOAP journal, and the fastest way to do SOAP on your phone.</p>

Most of us have had the same quiet-time problem: you read a chapter, it feels good in the moment, and by the afternoon you couldn't say what it was about. SOAP fixes that not by asking you to read more, but by making you respond to what you read. The method was popularized by pastor Wayne Cordeiro and has since become one of the most widely used devotional frameworks in the English-speaking church, largely because it's simple enough to do every morning without a study Bible or a seminary degree.

## What SOAP stands for

Four letters, four moves. Each one takes the passage one step closer to your actual life:

<ul class="article-check">
	<li><strong>S: Scripture.</strong> The verse or short passage you're focusing on today.</li>
	<li><strong>O: Observation.</strong> What the passage actually says, before you decide what it means for you.</li>
	<li><strong>A: Application.</strong> The one thing you'll do differently because of it.</li>
	<li><strong>P: Prayer.</strong> Your honest response, spoken back to God.</li>
</ul>

The order matters. Most of us jump straight from reading to "what does this mean for me," skipping the observation step entirely, which is how a verse ends up meaning whatever we already believed. SOAP slows that down.

## How to do a SOAP study, step by step

Pick a passage: a chapter you're working through, the reading from Sunday, or a single verse a friend sent you. Then work the four steps:

<ol class="article-steps">
	<li><strong>Scripture.</strong> Read the passage slowly, twice. Then write out, by hand if you can, the one verse that stood out. Copying it forces you to slow down and notice words you'd otherwise skim.</li>
	<li><strong>Observation.</strong> Ask what's actually happening. Who's speaking, to whom, and why? Note repeated words, contrasts ("but," "therefore"), commands, and promises. Stay descriptive here: you're reporting, not applying yet.</li>
	<li><strong>Application.</strong> Now ask "so what?" Turn one observation into a concrete change for this week, specific enough that you could act on it today, not a vague "trust God more."</li>
	<li><strong>Prayer.</strong> Close by praying the passage back to God. Thank him for what you saw, confess where you fall short of it, or ask for help to live it. Two honest sentences beat a paragraph of the right words.</li>
</ol>

## A worked example: SOAP on Proverbs 3:5-6

Reading the steps is one thing; seeing them done is another. Here's a full SOAP entry on a passage most believers know by heart:

<div class="soap-example">
	<p><strong>S: Scripture</strong><br />"Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths." Proverbs 3:5-6, KJV</p>
	<p><strong>O: Observation</strong><br />Trust here is "with <em>all</em> thine heart," total, not partial. It's paired with a negative command: don't lean on my own understanding. Then "in <em>all</em> thy ways," not just the obviously spiritual ones. The promise comes last and depends on the posture: he directs the paths of those who actually acknowledge him.</p>
	<p><strong>A: Application</strong><br />There's one decision this week I've been trying to reason my way through alone. Before I settle it, I'll stop and ask, "Am I leaning on my own understanding here?" Then bring it to God in prayer first, and tell one trusted friend, instead of deciding in my head and asking him to bless it after.</p>
	<p><strong>P: Prayer</strong><br />"Lord, I say I trust you, but I keep gripping this decision. Help me loosen my hold and lean on you instead of my own logic. Direct this path, and give me the humility to acknowledge you in it. Amen."</p>
</div>

Notice how the Application isn't "trust God more." It's a specific move tied to a specific decision. That's the difference between a study you remember and one you don't. The same idea works for Sunday's message too: see <a href="/blog/how-to-apply-a-sermon">how to apply a sermon to your life</a>.

## SOAP Bible Study Examples

One example shows the shape; a few more show how it bends to different kinds of passages: a psalm, words of Jesus, a letter, a command. All verses are quoted from the King James Version.

### SOAP example: Psalm 23:1

<div class="soap-example">
	<p><strong>S: Scripture</strong><br />"The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want." Psalm 23:1, KJV</p>
	<p><strong>O: Observation</strong><br />David was a shepherd himself, and here he takes the place of the sheep. It's personal: "<em>my</em> shepherd," not just "a shepherd." The verb is present tense, "is." And "I shall not want" doesn't mean I won't wish for things; it means I won't lack what I need. The rest of the psalm shows what that care looks like: rest, guidance, protection.</p>
	<p><strong>A: Application</strong><br />I check my bank app every morning before I pray. This week I'll reverse the order: before I open it, I'll write down three things I already have and thank God for them.</p>
	<p><strong>P: Prayer</strong><br />"Lord, you are my shepherd, not my paycheck. When I feel I'm lacking, remind me what you've already given. Amen."</p>
</div>

### SOAP example: John 15:5

<div class="soap-example">
	<p><strong>S: Scripture</strong><br />"I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." John 15:5, KJV</p>
	<p><strong>O: Observation</strong><br />Jesus says this to his disciples on the night before the cross. "Abide" keeps repeating in these verses. The branch doesn't try to produce fruit; it stays connected and the fruit comes. The last line is blunt: "without me ye can do <em>nothing</em>," not "less."</p>
	<p><strong>A: Application</strong><br />I treat time with God as something to fit in after the day starts. For the next five mornings I'll read and pray for five minutes before I open my phone, and see what changes.</p>
	<p><strong>P: Prayer</strong><br />"Jesus, I keep trying to grow on my own effort. Teach me to stay close to you first, and trust you with the fruit. Amen."</p>
</div>

### SOAP example: Philippians 4:6–7

<div class="soap-example">
	<p><strong>S: Scripture</strong><br />"Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God. And the peace of God, which passeth all understanding, shall keep your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus." Philippians 4:6-7, KJV</p>
	<p><strong>O: Observation</strong><br />In the KJV's English, "careful" means full of care, worried. Paul writes this while in chains (1:13). The answer to worry isn't "stop thinking about it" but prayer "in every thing," with thanksgiving attached. The promise isn't that the problem disappears; it's that God's peace will "keep," guard, my heart and mind.</p>
	<p><strong>A: Application</strong><br />When a worry comes up this week, I'll write it down as a one-line prayer, and add one thing I'm thankful for underneath it, before I do anything else about it.</p>
	<p><strong>P: Prayer</strong><br />"Father, here is what I'm anxious about today. Thank you for what you've already carried me through. Guard my heart with your peace. Amen."</p>
</div>

### SOAP example: James 1:22

<div class="soap-example">
	<p><strong>S: Scripture</strong><br />"But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves." James 1:22, KJV</p>
	<p><strong>O: Observation</strong><br />James says hearing without doing is self-deception, not just a missed opportunity. The next verses explain with a picture: a man looks in a mirror and immediately forgets what he looks like (1:23-24). The blessing is promised to the doer, "blessed in his deed" (1:25).</p>
	<p><strong>A: Application</strong><br />I'll pick one thing Sunday's sermon actually asked us to do and do it by Wednesday, then tell a friend I did it.</p>
	<p><strong>P: Prayer</strong><br />"Lord, I've heard a lot of good sermons and done little with them. Help me act on one thing this week. Amen."</p>
</div>

Across all four, the pattern is the same: the Observation stays with what the text says, and the Application names one specific action and when it will happen. If your applications keep coming out vague, that's the step to slow down on. Our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-apply-a-sermon">how to apply a sermon</a> works the same muscle for Sunday's message.

## Keeping a SOAP journal

A SOAP journal is simply your SOAP studies kept in one place, so you can see what God has been teaching you over months, not just today.

<ul class="article-check">
	<li><strong>One page per study.</strong> Date at the top, then the passage, then S, O, A and P. The printable template above is laid out exactly this way; a plain notebook works too.</li>
	<li><strong>Follow a plan.</strong> Working through one book of the Bible, a chapter a day, keeps you from spending ten minutes choosing a verse. The Gospel of John or Philippians are good first books.</li>
	<li><strong>Look back once a month.</strong> Read your last few Applications and ask which ones you actually did. That review is where the habit pays off.</li>
	<li><strong>Bring Sunday in.</strong> Use the sermon passage for Monday's study, so the message gets a second look. Our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-take-sermon-notes">how to take sermon notes</a> includes a free sermon notes template that pairs well with this one.</li>

## How to build a SOAP habit that lasts

<ul class="article-check">
	<li><strong>Keep it short.</strong> Ten minutes you do every day beats an hour you do twice. If your studies keep ballooning, you'll quietly stop. Shorter passages, honest sentences.</li>
	<li><strong>Same time, same place.</strong> Habits attach to cues. Tie SOAP to your first coffee, your commute, or the ten minutes before the house wakes up, and the trigger does the remembering for you.</li>
	<li><strong>Write, don't just think.</strong> The Application step only works if it's written down and specific. A thought evaporates; a written "I'll call my brother today" doesn't.</li>
	<li><strong>Let the sermon feed it.</strong> Sunday's passage is a ready-made week of SOAP. If you <a href="/blog/how-to-remember-sermons">review the sermon within a day</a>, you already have your Scripture and half your observations.</li>
</ul>

## SOAP vs other Bible study methods

SOAP isn't the only framework, and it isn't the deepest. Inductive study (observation, interpretation, application) goes further on hard passages; verse mapping and word studies dig into the original languages. What SOAP has over all of them is that you'll actually do it. The four steps are short enough to sustain daily, which is why it's a better *starting* method than a more thorough one you abandon in a week.
