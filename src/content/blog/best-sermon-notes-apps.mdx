<p class="article-intro">The best sermon note app for most people in 2026 is Sermon Keeper. It's the only one purpose-built to capture a sermon live, recording the audio while you take timestamped notes, with Bible verse autocomplete and an on-device AI transcript in a single flow. The other six apps below each win a narrower job: Sermon Scribe for post-service AI study guides, Church Notes for multiple Bible translations, Bible Note Journal for reflection and journaling, biblenote.ai for importing YouTube sermons, YouVersion Notes for free text notes, and Apple Notes for absolute simplicity.</p>

A few years ago, "sermon notes app" meant a general note-taking tool you happened to use in church. That's changed. There's now a whole category of apps built specifically for church and Bible study, and they're surprisingly different from one another. Some record and transcribe. Some are study-guide generators. Some are journals. Picking the right one comes down to <em>what you actually do</em> with a sermon: capture it as it happens, study it afterward, or simply jot a verse and a thought.

We organized this guide the way a tool roundup should work: one clear "best for" per app, an honest look at where each one fits, and a comparison table so you can scan it in ten seconds. Let's start with what separates a great sermon note app from a generic one.

## What to look for in a sermon note app

Most note apps can hold text. The ones built for sermons earn their place by solving the specific tension of being in a service. You want to be present and listening, not heads-down typing. As you compare options, these are the features that matter most:

<ul>
				<li><strong>Live capture during the service.</strong> Can you record the sermon as it's preached, so you're not relying on memory or frantic typing? This is the single biggest dividing line between apps.</li>
				<li><strong>Timestamped notes.</strong> When you mark a moment, does the app remember <em>when</em> it happened so you can jump back to that point in the audio later?</li>
				<li><strong>Bible verse autocomplete.</strong> Typing "John 3:16" should be instant, with no leaving the app to look it up.</li>
				<li><strong>AI transcription &amp; summary.</strong> Does it turn the recording into searchable text with the key points pulled out?</li>
				<li><strong>Works offline / on-device.</strong> Sanctuaries are notorious dead zones. On-device processing means it works without a signal, and keeps private notes and prayer requests off third-party servers.</li>
				<li><strong>Speed and simplicity in the moment.</strong> If setup takes longer than the sermon, you won't use it twice.</li>
			</ul>

No single app is the right answer for everyone, but only one app does all six of the above. Here's how the seven stack up.

<h2 id="sermon-keeper">1. Sermon Keeper: Best overall, built for live service capture <span style="white-space:nowrap;">&#9733; Winner</span></h2>

Sermon Keeper is the only app on this list designed around one moment: the sermon as it's being preached. Instead of asking you to choose between listening and writing, it records the audio while you take notes, and ties the two together automatically.

<figure class="article-figure">

				<figcaption>Tap to mark a moment and Sermon Keeper timestamps it against the live recording.</figcaption>
			</figure>

The point is being present in the service while still capturing everything. You hit record, listen, and tap or type whenever something lands, and each note is stamped to the exact second so you can jump straight back to it later. Type a reference like "Romans 8:28" and the verse arrives instantly. When the service ends, on-device AI produces a full transcript, a summary and the key points within minutes. Because all of that runs on the phone, it works with no signal in the building and your notes never leave the device.

A free 3-day trial includes timestamped notes and an AI transcript, with paid plans adding unlimited recordings and advanced features. It is the only option here that records, timestamps, transcribes and autocompletes verses in one flow, and you are recording within a tap or two of opening the app. The limits are worth naming: iOS only today, with Android in development, and it is a focused capture tool rather than a general journaling or study-guide builder. As a newer app, its community is still growing.

			<h2 id="sermon-scribe">2. Sermon Scribe: Best for post-service AI study guides</h2>

Sermon Scribe is built around what happens <em>after</em> the sermon. It takes a message and turns it into a structured AI study guide: summaries, discussion questions, and takeaways you can work through during the week or bring to a small group. That suits people whose main goal is deeper study rather than capturing the moment itself, and it is genuinely hands-off once you hand over the material. Small-group leaders who want ready-made discussion prompts get the most from it. It is sold as a subscription, with current pricing on the App Store.

Everything happens after the service, which is also the limitation. There is no live recording with timestamped notes while the pastor is speaking, no verse autocomplete as you write, and no on-device capture in the pew, so it cannot help you in a building with no signal. If you mainly want study guides built for you afterward, Sermon Scribe works well. For capturing the sermon live, see Sermon Keeper above.

<h2 id="church-notes">3. Church Notes: Best for multiple Bible translations</h2>

Church Notes is a note-taking app aimed squarely at Sunday morning, with a particular strength in working across many Bible translations. If comparing how a passage reads in different versions is part of how you take notes, that is where it shines. Quick access to a range of translations sits alongside a tidy, church-oriented layout, often with shareable templates for following a sermon outline, and it is pleasant to type structured notes into during a message. It is free to download with an optional paid upgrade.

Typed notes are the boundary. There is no live audio recording and no AI transcription to fall back on if you miss something, and nothing timestamps your notes against a recording. If switching between translations is central to how you study, Church Notes is a good home for typed notes. For recording the message live and getting a transcript, see Sermon Keeper above.

<h2 id="bible-note-journal">4. Bible Note Journal: Best for reflection and journaling</h2>

Bible Note Journal is less about capturing a sermon word-for-word and more about responding to it. It is a journaling app for devotional reflection: prompts, prayer, and a quiet space to write what God is teaching you. If your habit is to reflect and pray through what you heard rather than archive every point, it fits beautifully, and the warm, unhurried writing experience is good at building a consistent rhythm. It is free with optional in-app purchases.

The design is for reflection, not capture. There is no live sermon recording, no AI transcription, and no verse autocomplete tied to a recording, so you are writing from memory after the fact. If journaling and reflection are the point, Bible Note Journal is a lovely choice. If you also want the sermon itself as it is preached, see Sermon Keeper above.

			<h2 id="biblenote-ai">5. biblenote.ai: Best for importing YouTube sermons</h2>

biblenote.ai is built for sermons you watch online. Point it at a YouTube sermon and it works from the video, generating notes, transcripts and summaries from messages recorded somewhere else. That is genuinely useful if you get through a lot of sermons on video and would rather not watch every minute twice, and it removes the manual work of transcribing a recording yourself. There is a free tier, with a subscription for more.

It is oriented around imported video rather than the sermon happening in front of you, so there is no live in-room recording with timestamped notes, and it needs an internet connection to pull and process the video instead of working on-device in the pew. If most of your sermons come from YouTube, biblenote.ai is purpose-built for that. For the message you are hearing live on Sunday morning, see Sermon Keeper above.

<h2 id="youversion-notes">6. YouVersion Notes: Best free option for text notes</h2>

YouVersion Notes lives inside the YouVersion Bible app, the one already on millions of phones. It lets you jot notes and link them directly to verses as you read, entirely free, with nothing new to install if you already use it. Notes sync across your devices through your YouVersion account, and the tight integration with the Bible you are already reading makes it excellent for quick, verse-anchored thoughts.

It is a text-notes feature and nothing more. There is no audio recording, no AI transcription, and no timestamps linking notes to a recording, so if you fall behind during a fast section there is nothing to replay. For free, verse-linked text notes, YouVersion Notes is hard to beat. If you want to record and transcribe the sermon too, see Sermon Keeper above.

<h2 id="apple-notes">7. Apple Notes: Best for absolute simplicity</h2>

Apple Notes is the simplest option here. It is already on every iPhone and iPad, costs nothing, opens instantly, and asks nothing of you in setup or learning curve. If you want to open something familiar and start typing with zero friction, this is it: fast, synced across your Apple devices through iCloud, and perfectly fine for a few quick lines during a message.

It is a general notes app, not a sermon tool. There is no audio recording tied to your notes, no AI transcription, no verse autocomplete and no timestamps, and over time sermon notes get harder to find among grocery lists and reminders. If you want the absolute simplest thing and already think in plain text, Apple Notes does the job. When you are ready to actually capture and revisit the sermon, see Sermon Keeper above.

## Which sermon note app is right for you?

The "best" app depends on the one job you most want done:

<ul>
				<li><strong>You want to capture the sermon live and revisit it later</strong>, with recording, timestamped notes, verse autocomplete, and a transcript in one flow. That's Sermon Keeper.</li>
				<li><strong>You mainly study after the service</strong> and want an AI-built study guide: Sermon Scribe.</li>
				<li><strong>You compare Bible translations</strong> as you take typed notes: Church Notes.</li>
				<li><strong>Sermon notes are a devotional, journaling habit</strong> for you: Bible Note Journal.</li>
				<li><strong>You mostly watch sermons on YouTube</strong> and want notes from the video: biblenote.ai.</li>
				<li><strong>You want free, verse-linked text notes</strong> inside your Bible app: YouVersion Notes.</li>
				<li><strong>You want the simplest possible thing</strong>, already on your phone: Apple Notes.</li>
			</ul>

For most churchgoers, the deciding factor is being able to stay present in the service without losing the message. That's the gap Sermon Keeper was built to close: record it live, take timestamped notes, autocomplete your verses, and walk away with a transcript, all without an internet connection.
