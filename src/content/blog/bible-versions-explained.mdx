<p class="article-intro">Bible versions differ in two ways: how closely they follow the original Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek wording, and which ancient manuscripts they translate from. Word-for-word versions like the NASB, ESV and KJV keep close to the original sentence; thought-for-thought versions like the NIV and NLT put the meaning into natural modern English. None of them is a different Bible. They are different ways of bringing the same text into English.</p>

If you've ever sat in church reading along while the pastor's words didn't quite match the page in front of you, this is why. This guide explains where the differences come from, puts the same verses side by side so you can see them, and helps you choose a translation you'll actually read.

## Why there are so many Bible versions

The Bible wasn't written in English. The Old Testament is mostly Hebrew, with some Aramaic, and the New Testament is Greek. Every English Bible is a translation, and translators have to make three kinds of decisions.

<ul class="article-check">
	<li><strong>How literal to be.</strong> Hebrew and Greek don't build sentences the way English does. A translator can keep the original structure and ask the reader to work a little, or rebuild the sentence so it reads naturally.</li>
	<li><strong>Which manuscripts to follow.</strong> We don't have the original documents, but thousands of handwritten copies. Many of the oldest were found after the King James Version was made, and where the copies differ, translations sometimes choose differently.</li>
	<li><strong>What English sounds like today.</strong> Language keeps moving. Words that were clear in 1611 can mean something else now, so every generation revises or starts fresh.</li>
</ul>

Put those together and you get dozens of faithful translations that still sound different from each other.

## Word-for-word, thought-for-thought, paraphrase

The most useful way to understand the versions is as a spectrum.

<ul>
	<li><strong>Word-for-word</strong> (formal equivalence): follows the original words and word order as closely as English allows. NASB, ESV, KJV and NKJV sit here. They are good for close study, and a little harder to read.</li>
	<li><strong>Thought-for-thought</strong> (dynamic equivalence): translates the meaning of each phrase into natural English. The NIV sits close to the middle; the NLT goes further toward everyday language. They are easier to read in long stretches.</li>
	<li><strong>Paraphrase:</strong> one writer retells the text in contemporary speech. The Message by Eugene Peterson is the best known. It can make a familiar passage feel new, but it is best read alongside a translation rather than instead of one.</li>
</ul>

The CSB describes its own approach as "optimal equivalence," which places it between the ESV and the NIV.

Differences are easier to see than to describe. Here are the same verses in six English translations, exactly as they appear in each.

The meaning is the same in every row. What changes is one Greek word, <em>monogenēs</em>. Older translations render it "only begotten"; many modern ones read it as "only" or "one and only," meaning unique. You can also see the NKJV and NASB capitalising "He" and "Him" when they refer to God, a choice the ESV and NIV don't make.

In 1611, "charity" meant self-giving love, from the Latin <em>caritas</em>. Today it mostly means donating to a cause, so every modern translation says "love." This is the clearest example of why the NKJV exists: same text, updated English.

## Why some verses are missing

Turn to Acts 8:37 in an ESV or an NIV and you'll find the number skipped, with a footnote. The King James prints the verse; the NASB 1995 keeps it in square brackets. The same happens with a handful of others, such as Matthew 17:21.

The reason is manuscripts. The KJV's New Testament, and the NKJV's, follow the Greek text printed in the 1500s, known as the Textus Receptus. Most modern translations follow older copies found since, and those copies don't include these verses. Translators didn't cut anything; they moved the words to a footnote so you can see what the later copies added. Our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-read-bible-references">how to read Bible references</a> covers the other reason numbers can shift between Bibles.

## Which Bible translation should you read?

The honest answer is the one you'll read. Beyond that, match the translation to how you'll use it.

<ul class="article-check">
	<li><strong>You're new to the Bible:</strong> start with the NLT or the NIV. They read like modern English, so you can follow a whole chapter without stopping.</li>
	<li><strong>You want to study closely:</strong> the ESV or the NASB keeps the original sentence structure, which helps when a sermon or commentary leans on a single word.</li>
	<li><strong>You grew up with the King James:</strong> the NKJV keeps the familiar phrasing and rhythm without the "thee" and "thou."</li>
	<li><strong>You memorize Scripture:</strong> choose one translation and stay with it. Switching versions is the fastest way to muddle a verse you almost know.</li>
	<li><strong>You want to follow the sermon:</strong> read what your church reads. It makes it far easier to keep your place while the pastor is speaking.</li>
	<li><strong>You read in Spanish:</strong> the Reina-Valera family is the traditional Protestant choice; the 1960 revision is the most widely used today, and the older 1909 edition is in the public domain.</li>
</ul>

Many people end up with two: an easy-reading translation for daily reading and a more literal one for study. That's a good arrangement. Reading a difficult verse in two translations often clears it up faster than a commentary would.

### A note on Catholic and Orthodox Bibles

Protestant Bibles have 66 books. Catholic Bibles have 73, adding books such as Tobit, Judith and 1 and 2 Maccabees, and Orthodox Bibles include a few more. Catholic readers in the United States will usually meet the New American Bible, the translation the readings at Mass are based on; its current edition is the NABRE. The translations in the chart above are Protestant editions.

## Which translation is your pastor using?

Most churches tell you somewhere: the pew Bible, the corner of the screen where the verse is projected, the bulletin or the church website. If not, listen for clues.

<ul>
	<li>"Thee," "thou" and "-eth" endings mean the KJV.</li>
	<li>"Only begotten Son" in John 3:16 points to the KJV, the NKJV or the NASB; "one and only Son" to the NIV.</li>
	<li>"Charity" in 1 Corinthians 13 is the KJV; "love" is everything since.</li>
</ul>

## Putting it together

Every major translation is a serious attempt to bring the same Scripture into English. The differences come down to how literally each one translates, which manuscripts it follows, and how old its English is. Pick one that suits how you read, keep a second one nearby for hard passages, and read what your church reads on Sunday.
