<p class="article-intro">To apply a sermon to your life, choose one thing the preacher asked for, turn it into a specific action with a when and a where, and come back to it midweek. Most sermons don't fail to change us because they were weak. They fade because we leave with a good feeling instead of a next step, and by Tuesday the feeling is gone.</p>

Almost every guide on sermon application is written for preachers: how to write the application section of a message. This one is for the rest of us, the people in the seats who want Sunday to still matter on Thursday.

## Why sermons fade by Tuesday

James describes it exactly:

> For if any be a hearer of the word, and not a doer, he is like unto a man beholding his natural face in a glass: For he beholdeth himself, and goeth his way, and straightway forgetteth what manner of man he was. <cite>James 1:23–24 (KJV)</cite>

You see something true in the mirror, you walk away, and it's gone. Jesus ends the Sermon on the Mount with the same warning: the wise builder is the one who "heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them" (Matthew 7:24, KJV). Hearing and doing are two different steps, and the second one rarely happens by accident.

There's a practical reason too. A sermon is thirty or forty minutes of ideas delivered once, and memory holds on to very little of what we hear only once. Without a way to carry it into the week, even a great sermon becomes a vague memory of "it was good."

## 7 steps to apply a sermon

### 1. Listen for the "so what"

Most sermons contain a moment where the preacher turns from explaining the passage to asking something of the congregation. Listen for it. It often sounds like "so what does this mean for us," "this week, I want you to," or "if you're here and you've been…"

Write it down in the preacher's words, not yours. "Call the person you've been avoiding" is more useful later than "reconciliation." If you want a simple framework for this, our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-take-sermon-notes">how to take notes in church</a> shows where to put it on the page.

### 2. Choose one thing

A good sermon can leave you with five things to change. Trying all five usually means doing none. Pick the one that touches your actual week: your work, your family, a conversation you've been putting off, a habit you already know about. The rest can wait. There's another sermon next Sunday.

### 3. Make it specific

This is where most applications fall apart. "Pray more," "be more patient" and "trust God" are true, but you can't tell on Friday whether you did them. Turn the general into the specific with three questions: who, what and when?

A good test: could a friend ask you on Saturday, "Did you do it?" and get a yes or no? If so, it's specific enough.

### 4. Tie it to a moment

A new action sticks best when it's attached to something you already do. Psychologists call these "implementation intentions": plans in the form "when X happens, I will do Y." In practice it sounds like this:

<ul class="article-check">
	<li>When I start the car in the morning, I'll pray for my manager.</li>
	<li>When I put my phone on the charger at night, I'll write down one worry as a prayer.</li>
	<li>When I sit down to lunch on Wednesday, I'll reread the passage.</li>
</ul>

The moment does the remembering for you, so the step doesn't depend on willpower.

### 5. Come back to it midweek

Wednesday is the point where Sunday usually slips away, so plan to return to it then. Reread the main passage, look at your notes, and ask how your one step is going. Ten minutes is plenty. The <a href="/blog/soap-bible-study-method">SOAP method</a> (Scripture, Observation, Application, Prayer) is a simple structure for exactly this, and its "A" is the step you chose on Sunday.

### 6. Tell someone

Say your step out loud to a spouse, a friend or your small group: "The sermon was on forgiveness, and I'm going to message my brother this week." It turns a private resolution into a shared one, and it gives someone permission to ask you about it. If your church has small groups, the sermon is often the discussion topic anyway.

### 7. Look back next Sunday

Before the next sermon starts, take a minute to ask what happened with last week's step. Maybe you did it. Maybe you did it once and forgot. Maybe it was the wrong step. All three are useful to know. Carry forward what's worth keeping, and let the rest go without guilt. Growth over a year comes from many small steps, not a perfect streak.

## When a sermon doesn't seem to apply

Some Sundays, the sermon is mostly about who God is: His holiness, His faithfulness, the work of Christ. It can feel like there's nothing to do. There is, but it may not be a task.

<ul>
	<li><strong>Believe something.</strong> The application may be to trust a promise you've been doubting. Name the promise and the situation.</li>
	<li><strong>Worship.</strong> Thank God for the specific thing the sermon showed you about Him, by name, each day this week.</li>
	<li><strong>Pray for someone else.</strong> If the sermon speaks to a situation you're not in, ask who you know that is in it, and pray for them.</li>
</ul>

And sometimes the honest answer is that this week's sermon wasn't for you. That's fine. Keep the notes; they may matter more in six months than they do today.

## Sermon application questions

Keep these on a card in your Bible or in a note on your phone. You don't need all of them each week; two or three are enough.

<ul class="article-check">
	<li>What is the one main idea of this sermon, in a sentence?</li>
	<li>What did the passage mean to the people who first heard it?</li>
	<li>Is there a promise to trust, a command to obey or a warning to heed?</li>
	<li>What did the preacher specifically ask us to do?</li>
	<li>Where in my week does this touch my actual life?</li>
	<li>What is one thing I will do, and when?</li>
	<li>Who could I tell, or who could I pray for?</li>
	<li>What would be different next Sunday if I did this?</li>
</ul>

## A worked example

Say the sermon is on Philippians 4:6: "Be careful for nothing; but in every thing by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God" (KJV). Here's how the steps play out.

<ul>
	<li><strong>The "so what":</strong> the pastor says, "Don't carry your worries around all week; turn each one into a prayer as soon as you notice it."</li>
	<li><strong>One thing:</strong> the worry about a medical test on Friday.</li>
	<li><strong>Specific:</strong> each night this week, write that worry as a one-line prayer, with one thing I'm thankful for underneath.</li>
	<li><strong>The moment:</strong> when I plug in my phone at night.</li>
	<li><strong>Midweek:</strong> on Wednesday, reread Philippians 4:4–9 and notice verse 7, the promise of peace.</li>
	<li><strong>Tell someone:</strong> ask a friend to pray for me on Friday morning.</li>
	<li><strong>Next Sunday:</strong> look back at the week's prayers and what happened.</li>
</ul>

It takes a couple of minutes a day. By Friday, the sermon has become something you lived, not just something you heard.

## Putting it together

Applying a sermon isn't about acting on everything you heard. It's about leaving with one specific step, attaching it to a moment in your week, returning to it on Wednesday and looking back on Sunday. Do that most weeks and, over a year, fifty sermons become fifty small changes.
