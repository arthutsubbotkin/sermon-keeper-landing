## Which Bible reading plan should you choose?

- **The New Testament plan** is the best first plan. A chapter takes about five minutes, you meet Jesus in the Gospels in the first months, and the weekends give you room to catch up. If you've tried a Bible-in-a-year plan before and stopped in Leviticus, start here.
- **The whole-Bible plan** is for when you're ready for 10 to 15 minutes a day. It reads the books in the order of your Bible, from Genesis to Revelation. Instead of a fixed number of chapters, each day has a similar amount of text: some days are four short chapters, and Psalm 119, the longest chapter in the Bible, gets a day to itself.
- **A chronological plan** puts the Bible in the order events happened, for example reading Job near Genesis and the Psalms alongside David's life. Many people love it, but scholars disagree on parts of the order, so we haven't made one we can't check. If you want one, ask your church which plan it uses, so you can read along with others.

If your church is reading through a book together this year, the best plan is the one they're using. Reading alongside other people makes it much easier to keep going.

## How to stick with a Bible reading plan in January

Starting on January 1 is easy. Keeping going once the new-year energy fades is the hard part. A few simple habits help:

1. **Same time, same place.** Attach the reading to something you already do every day: the first coffee, the train, lunch. "When I sit down with coffee, I open my Bible" works better than "I'll read sometime today."
2. **Keep the PDF where you'll see it.** Tape it inside your Bible or next to the coffee machine. A row of ticked boxes is a small daily win, and it's easier to keep a streak going when you can see it.
3. **Read with someone.** Pick the same plan as a friend, your spouse or your small group, and send each other one verse a day. You'll keep going for them on the days you wouldn't for yourself.
4. **Let Sunday help.** Listen for connections between the sermon and what you read during the week, and bring a question from your reading to church.
5. **Aim for understanding, not speed.** If a chapter grips you, stop and think about it. The plan is a guide, not a race.

If you'd like the Bible's own picture of a daily habit, Psalm 1 describes the person whose "delight is in the law of the Lord; and in his law doth he meditate day and night" (Psalm 1:2, KJV).

## What to do when you fall behind

You will miss days. Everyone does. What matters is what you do next:

- **Skip to today's date.** Read today's reading, tick it, and keep going. Don't try to read five days in one sitting.
- **Catch up at the weekend, or let it go.** On the New Testament plan the weekends are already free. On the whole-Bible plan, use a quiet Saturday to read one or two missed days. If you can't, leave the boxes empty and finish them in December, or next year.
- **Don't restart from January 1.** Starting over means you're always behind, and that's discouraging. A plan with gaps that you finish is better than a perfect plan you abandon.
- **Switch plans if you need to.** If the whole-Bible plan is too much this season, move to the New Testament plan at today's date. That's not failing. It's choosing a pace you can keep.

## How to take notes on what you read

Reading is good. Reading and writing one or two lines is better, because writing makes you slow down and remember. You don't need a long journal entry. Try this after each reading:

- **One verse** that stood out, copied out in full with the reference.
- **One sentence** about what it shows you about God, or about people.
- **One step** you'll take today because of it.
