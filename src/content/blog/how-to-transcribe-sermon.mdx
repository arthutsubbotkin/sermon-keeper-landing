## Free ways to transcribe a sermon

### YouTube's built-in transcript

Many churches stream or upload their services to YouTube, and YouTube creates automatic captions with speech recognition. To read them as one text, open the video, expand the description and click **Show transcript**. Google's help page says you can see the full transcript of any video that has captions, and you can click a line to jump to that moment.

Two catches. Automatic captions can be wrong, and YouTube itself warns they may misrepresent what was said because of accents or background noise. And not every video has them: captions may not be ready right after upload, and YouTube lists videos that are too long or have poor sound among the reasons they may be missing. For livestreams, automatic live captions are English-only, and new captions are created after the stream ends.

### Voice Memos or Notes on iPhone

If you record the sermon yourself on an iPhone 12 or later, Apple's own apps can transcribe it. In Voice Memos, tap a recording and choose **View Transcript** or **Copy Transcript**; you can even watch the words appear while you record. In Notes, start an audio recording inside a note and add the transcript to the note. Apple says transcription works in English, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, German, Japanese, Korean and Chinese, but isn't available in every country or region. You get plain text, with no summary or Bible references pulled out. For the recording itself, see our guide to <a href="/blog/how-to-record-sermon-on-iphone">recording a sermon on iPhone</a>.

### Free AI transcription tools

Free plans are fine for an occasional sermon, but read the limits; most sermons run longer than 30 minutes.

- **TurboScribe:** 3 files a day free, each up to 30 minutes, one upload at a time and at lower priority. It accepts common audio and video files (MP3, M4A, WAV, MP4 and more) and lists YouTube among supported inputs.
- **Otter:** the free Basic plan has 300 transcription minutes a month, but a maximum of 30 minutes per conversation and only 3 file imports for the lifetime of the account.
- **Notta:** the free plan has 120 minutes a month but transcribes only up to 3 minutes per recording, so it isn't practical for whole sermons.

A 40-minute sermon on a 30-minute free plan means splitting the file in two, or upgrading for a month.

## Human sermon transcription services

If the transcript will be published on your church website, turned into a book or handed out in print, a person should type or at least proofread it. Human services cost far more and take longer, but they check names and Scripture references.

- **Rev:** $1.99 per minute, delivered in 12 hours or less, with a stated 99%+ accuracy guarantee and rush options.
- **GMR Transcription:** has a sermon page with US-based human transcriptionists; its general rates for clear audio run from $1.25 a minute (3–4 weeks) to $3.50 a minute (same day).
- **SermonScribe:** a US service that has transcribed sermons since 2008. $3.99 per audio minute on demand (they estimate about $160 per sermon) or $3.59 for a weekly service, with a 99.9% accuracy promise. They lightly edit the text and check Scripture references, so it isn't word-for-word.

For a 40-minute sermon, that's roughly $50 to $160, depending on the service and turnaround.

## How accurate is a sermon transcript?

Accuracy numbers on vendors' sites are their own claims, measured on their own test audio. Rev, for example, states 95%+ for its AI transcription and 99%+ for human work. Your result depends mostly on the recording:

- **Audio quality is the biggest factor.** The church's own soundboard recording is usually far cleaner than a phone in the back row.
- **Bible names and references are the weak spot.** Names like Nebuchadnezzar and references like "Romans 8:28" are where automatic transcripts most often go wrong. Proofread them against your Bible.
- **Music and crowd noise confuse speech recognition.** If you record yourself, start when the sermon begins, not with the worship set.
- **Accents and fast speech** lower accuracy for every tool, human or AI.

If you're unsure how a reference should be written, our guide on <a href="/blog/how-to-read-bible-references">how to read Bible references</a> explains the format.

## How long does it take?

- **YouTube transcript:** instant, if captions already exist.
- **AI tools:** usually minutes; Rev says its AI transcripts arrive in five minutes or less. Free plans may queue you behind paying users.
- **Sermon Keeper:** up to 15 minutes for a long recording, by the app's own estimate.
- **Human services:** from 12 hours (Rev) to days or weeks, depending on the turnaround you pay for.

## Step by step: how to transcribe a sermon

<ol class="article-steps">
	<li><strong>Get the best audio you can.</strong> Ask your church whether it has the audio file; many post sermons as podcasts. If you record yourself, sit near a speaker and start at the sermon, not the music.</li>
	<li><strong>Check for a free transcript.</strong> If the sermon is on YouTube, try "Show transcript" first.</li>
	<li><strong>Pick a tool for your situation.</strong> Free AI for an occasional sermon under 30 minutes, a paid AI plan or a sermon app for weekly use, a human service for anything you'll publish.</li>
	<li><strong>Upload or record.</strong> Upload the file, or record live in an app that transcribes afterward.</li>
	<li><strong>Proofread names and references.</strong> Check every Bible name, book and verse number before you share it.</li>
	<li><strong>Use it.</strong> Search it, pull out the main points for your <a href="/blog/how-to-take-sermon-notes">sermon notes</a>, or share it with your small group.</li>
</ol>

## Before you publish a transcript

A transcript for your own study is one thing; publishing someone else's sermon is another. Ask the pastor or church before you post or print a transcript, even with credit.
